BLACKWOOD ARCHIVE / HOLDFAST-04

The listening station stood at the edge of the headland where the cliffs fell sharply into the North Sea, a low concrete structure sunk into black rock and weathered by decades of salt wind. Lichen spread across the outer walls in dark blooms. Above the roof, the aerial mast hummed faintly through the foundations whenever the weather turned offshore, long before storms reached land. Beyond the station, the sea shifted beneath a restless sky, steel grey breaking briefly into dull blue before another bank of cloud moved east across the water and drew the colour away.

Inside, the receivers filled the building with a continuous mechanical hum that settled gradually into the body after enough hours beside it. The sound occupied both rooms and the narrow corridor between them, threading through shelving units stacked with labelled archive reels, maintenance manuals, spare valves, and logbooks whose paper edges had yellowed beneath years of damp air. Warm circuitry lingered above the cold rising from the stone floor, and dust gathered at the back of the throat with the taste of metal.

Gareth Ellison sat at the central desk with a headset resting over one ear, his chair angled towards the primary receiver bank while a sharpened pencil lay across the open logbook beside his hand. He listened with the ease of long habit, tracking frequencies the way other men followed weather or tide. Most shifts passed in repetition: trawlers out of Aberdeen trading weather reports and arguments over quotas, cargo vessels confirming headings in clipped exchanges, coastguard relays crossing the channels in bursts of procedure and static. Even atmospheric drift from distant frequencies carried familiar structures once it cleared enough to resolve into speech. Gareth logged each transmission in steady handwriting because the work rewarded patience more than speed, and silence had a way of encouraging inexperienced operators towards mistakes.

Late that morning, while moving through the mid-band frequencies, he allowed a stretch of static to continue longer than usual. His fingers rested lightly against the tuning dial while the hiss filled the headset. A pattern moved beneath the interference, faint at first and then regular enough to keep his hand in place. He adjusted the tuning slowly. Resistance dragged beneath his fingertips before the signal steadied and a woman’s voice emerged through the noise in a calm, even cadence: “…you are hearing this after it has already begun. Keep listening.”

Gareth leaned forward until one shoulder touched the edge of the desk. His attention shifted immediately to the receiver display, where no identifier appeared, no call sign followed the transmission, and the signal strength held perfectly stable across the channel. “You will remember me differently each time you play this. Something will shift, and you will think it always sounded that way.”

His pencil moved across the page: Female voice. Unverified source. No identification. Content irregular.

Rain struck somewhere outside while the receivers continued their low electrical hum. “Someone will be gone, and you won’t notice when it happens.”

The pencil slowed. The voice remained unnaturally close inside the headset, intimate enough to alter his sense of the room, while static drifted beneath the words without obscuring them. The transmission lasted less than three minutes, and throughout it the woman never identified herself or named a destination. Pronouns moved through the message without attaching themselves firmly to any person Gareth could picture.

The final sentence arrived lower than the rest: “This isn’t your voice.” A brief crackle followed before the channel dissolved back into static.

Gareth removed the headset and looked towards the clock mounted above the shelving. The clock read 10:42. He wrote the time beside the tape counter where the reel-to-reel unit had recorded the transmission automatically, then rewound the tape and replayed the final section while checking the wording against his notes. The sentence arrived exactly as before: “This isn’t your voice.”

Beneath the entry he copied the line: This isn’t my voice.

The wording looked ordinary enough once it sat on the page. He capped the pencil, rewound the reel again, and placed it inside a labelled archive case marked Pending Review. Outside, the storm finally reached the headland. Rain battered the narrow windows while the mast above the station released a long metallic groan as the wind shifted across the cliff face.

By early afternoon, the weather had settled fully over the coast. Inside the station, the equipment maintained its steady rhythm while Gareth entered the anomaly into the central archive system and aligned the written log with the digital recording. The reception timestamp displayed 10:42. The originating transmission code displayed a timestamp from forty-eight hours earlier.

He stared at the screen while the discrepancy held exact to the minute.

The station clock matched the national time server. The archive file verified as clean. No relay markers appeared in the transmission data. No replay delay had been introduced by the system. No carrier corruption showed anywhere in the diagnostics.

The broadcast had entered the receiver live while carrying a timestamp from two days earlier.

The equipment hummed steadily around him while diagnostic reports scrolled across the monitor. Gareth sat back in his chair and looked again at the originating code. In twenty years at the station, he had never seen a discrepancy survive verification so completely. Eventually he reached for the tape case beside him, threaded the reel back into the machine, advanced it carefully to the counter mark recorded in the logbook, and settled the headphones over both ears.

Static rolled through the line before the woman’s voice returned: “…you are hearing this after it has already begun. Keep listening.”

Gareth followed the wording silently in the notebook resting open on the desk. After several seconds, he realised his eyes were moving ahead of the recording itself, anticipating phrases before they arrived. “You will remember me differently each time you play this. Something will shift, and you will think it always sounded that way.”

The next sentence approached. “Someone will be gone, and you won’t notice when it happens.”

He kept his eyes fixed on the page. “This isn’t your voice.”

The tape rolled onward into static before stopping with a hard mechanical click that carried sharply through the room.

Gareth left the headphones on for several moments while the empty channel pressed faintly against his ears. When he finally lowered the headset around his neck and looked back at the notebook, his attention settled immediately on the line beneath the entry: This isn’t my voice.

The handwriting remained neat and even across the page. He remembered writing it. He remembered hearing the sentence through the headphones. Yet the order of those events resisted arrangement. The line carried the strange weight of an older notation, absorbed into the notebook long before the transmission reached the station.

He rewound the tape and replayed the final seconds: “This isn’t your voice.”

The cadence remained unchanged.

He checked the tape counter against the notation in the logbook, opened the digital archive, isolated the final sentence, and routed the playback through the external speakers: “This isn’t your voice.”

The voice faded, leaving the station to settle once more into machinery, rain, and distant sea. Gareth looked back towards the notebook and tried to reconstruct the sequence clearly: the transmission arriving through the receiver, the replay, the line written beneath the entry. The events refused to arrange themselves. Each time he thought he had recovered the order, the memory shifted slightly and returned him to uncertainty.

For a long while he sat motionless at the desk, listening to the weather move across the headland while the tape remained threaded through the machine and the notebook stayed open before him. At last, he reached for the pencil again and drew a line beneath the entry without adding another word, leaving the sentence isolated above it while the station continued humming quietly around him.

The Third Night

They reached the turn-off later than expected, though neither of them thought of it as a mistake at the time. Callum blamed the light. The cloud had come down earlier than forecast, flattening the hills and making distance harder to judge. Jamie blamed the map, which was old and softened by use, its creases worn pale where it had been folded and unfolded too many times. They didn’t argue about it. They rarely did. Callum had learned, after his wife died, to let some things go, and Jamie had learned that his father listened better when he wasn’t being pushed.

They stood where the track thinned and gave way to grass, both of them looking upslope longer than necessary.

“This is it,” Callum said, though he wasn’t fully certain. The cairn was there, just visible through the mist, but smaller than the photograph in the guidebook had suggested. Everything seemed smaller once you were actually standing in front of it, stripped of scale and context.

Jamie adjusted his pack as he listened. He was sixteen, all elbows and narrow shoulders, still growing into his frame, and the pack looked too big on him even though Callum knew the weight was right because he had checked it himself.

“You sure?” Jamie asked.

Callum nodded. “We’re just running a bit late.”

They had planned carefully. Two nights out, camp near the loch the first evening, an early start over the ridge, the second night at the bothy, then a loop back to the car. It wasn’t a hard route, just long enough to feel earned and just demanding enough to matter.

Jamie hadn’t pushed back when Callum suggested it, which had surprised him at the time. Since his mother’s death two years earlier, Jamie had become cautious in a way that wasn’t fear exactly, but sat close to it. He double-checked things. He asked questions he hadn’t used to ask. He watched Callum more closely, as if quietly measuring whether the rules he’d grown up with still applied.

The hills had been Callum’s idea. They always were. Walking had been the one thing he and his wife had shared without effort or negotiation. There had been no need for conversation, just movement and weather and the shared understanding that silence didn’t need to be filled.

Jamie remembered fragments of those trips. Cold mornings, wet socks, the way his mother laughed when the stove refused to light. He remembered her humming under her breath, tuneless but steady. He couldn’t recall the tune.

They climbed steadily. As the path narrowed, the wind sharpened along the ridge, cutting across their faces and working its way into gaps in clothing. Jamie pulled his hood tight, his breath showing pale against the cold as he kept pace.

Callum set the rhythm without thinking about it, falling into the kind of steady, measured pace that came from years of walking alone. He checked back every so often, counting steps between glances out of habit. Jamie followed easily. When they reached the top, the ground fell away on the far side. The loch lay below them, dark and still, its surface barely disturbed. Beyond it, the valley curved inward, the land folding in on itself in a way that made distances difficult to judge.

“That’s where we’ll camp,” Callum said, pointing.

Jamie followed the line of his finger and frowned slightly. “I thought it’d be closer.”

“It always looks farther from up here,” Callum said.

Jamie nodded, though his gaze lingered on the loch longer than necessary before he turned away.

They pitched the tent as the light thinned toward evening. The ground near the loch was damp but firm, the moss thick enough to hold pegs without trouble. Callum showed Jamie how to angle the tent into the wind, even though Jamie already knew. He’d learned quickly over the last two years, not because he loved it, but because competence felt safer than relying on someone else.

They ate sitting on stones with their backs turned to the breeze, freeze-dried pasta straight from the packets. Callum watched the sky while Jamie ate, noting how the clouds dragged low and fast, their undersides darkening.

“Could rain overnight,” Callum said.

Jamie shrugged. “Tent’ll hold.”

“It will,” Callum agreed.

They didn’t talk much after that. Callum lay awake longer than he meant to, listening to Jamie’s breathing even after it settled into sleep. The sound anchored him in place. At some point during the night, he woke with the sense that something had moved outside the tent. He stayed still until the sound faded and told himself, firmly, that it wasn’t worth waking a sixteen-year-old over nothing he couldn’t be sure of.

Morning came cold and colourless, frost glittering on the ground. Jamie crawled out of the tent rubbing his eyes.

“You hear anything last night?” he asked, keeping his tone casual.

Callum hesitated just long enough to notice it, then shook his head. “Just the wind.”

Jamie accepted that answer without comment.

They packed quickly. The climb toward the ridge took longer than expected, the slope steeper than it had looked from below. Jamie moved ahead this time, his stride lengthening as he warmed up.

Halfway up, he stopped and waited for Callum to reach him before pointing down at the ground.

There were boot prints, old ones mostly filled with ice, their edges softened but readable.

“Probably from last week,” Callum said after a moment.

“They’re going the same way we are,” Jamie said.

Callum nodded. Jamie straightened and said nothing more.

They reached the ridge before midday. The wind cut hard across it, strong enough to sting. The bothy took longer to spot than Callum expected, and when he finally saw it, he had to stop and look twice.

“There,” Jamie said.

The building sat low against the hillside, half sunk into the slope. Stone walls, slate roof, a single dark doorway.

“Looks smaller,” Jamie said.

Callum nodded. “It does.”

The descent was careful work. Frost glazed the grass in places, turning it slick. Callum went first, planting his poles deliberately, while Jamie followed a few steps behind, quieter than before.

The door stuck when Callum tried it, then opened with a dull scrape. Inside, the air was cold and still, smelling of damp stone and old ash. A bench ran along one wall. The stove squatted in the corner.

“Good enough,” Callum said, more out of habit than enthusiasm.

Jamie looked around, his gaze lingering on the walls. “People carved stuff.”

Callum nodded. Names, dates, marks left by people who wanted proof they’d been there. They dropped their packs, and Callum stacked what wood he could find before coaxing the stove into life. The flame caught.

Jamie stood near the door for a moment. “Thought I heard something.”

“Wind,” Callum said, and wanted it to be true.

That night, the knocking came, three taps spaced far enough apart to be distinct.

Nothing followed.

They slept on opposite sides of the room. Callum stayed awake, watching the ceiling beams while the stove ticked softly as it cooled.

Morning came slowly. Frost traced delicate patterns along the inside of the window.

Callum stepped outside first. The ground was white and smooth.

“No tracks,” Jamie said behind him.

Their own prints from the night before were faint but visible. There were no others.

“The wind must’ve covered them,” Callum said, though he heard how thin it sounded.

The path didn’t line up with what Callum expected. After twenty minutes, Callum stopped and pulled out the map, then the compass, checking both.

“This isn’t right,” he said.

Jamie stood beside him. “We’re too low.”

They kept walking. The valley narrowed instead of opening.

“Dad,” Jamie said quietly. “I think we’ve been here before.”

Callum recognised the rock outcrop, split cleanly down the middle.

“That’s not possible,” he said.

Jamie reached into his pocket and pulled out a strip of orange tape. It was tied around the stone.

“We’ll go back to the bothy.”

The building came into view sooner than it should have.

The door was open.

“I thought I closed it,” Callum said.

Jamie stepped back. “I don’t think we should stay.”

Callum turned to answer him, the knocking came again, and this time it came from inside the walls.

The sound didn’t repeat, and that absence unsettled Callum more than the knock itself, because repetition could be explained, while silence left him with nothing to argue against.

Jamie stood very still near the centre of the room, one hand half-raised as if he’d been about to reach for something and stopped himself mid-motion, caught between instinct and uncertainty.

“It’s the stove,” Callum said, forcing the words out with more confidence than he felt. “Metal shifts when it cools.”

Jamie didn’t answer, his eyes fixed on the corner of the room where the sound had seemed to come from.

Callum crossed the space between them and put his hand against the stove door, keeping his movements slow and deliberate, as though sudden motion might make things worse. The metal was warm beneath his palm, not hot enough to burn, but warm enough that it didn’t make sense.

“That’s not...” Jamie started, his voice catching before the thought could finish forming.

“I know,” Callum said, more sharply than he meant to, and he pulled his hand away, flexing his fingers as if the heat had stung him. “We must not have noticed it was still going. Or it caught again somehow.”

Jamie leaned closer to the firebox, watching the flame inside, which burned low and steady, the way it did when it had been fed recently rather than left to die on its own.

“Dad,” he said, quieter now, as though volume itself might provoke something. “We didn’t leave anything burning.”

Callum opened his mouth to argue, then stopped, because the facts refused to line up cleanly enough to support the lie. Instead, he forced himself to breathe slowly, counting each breath the way he’d been taught years ago, long before Jamie was born, back when panic had felt like something that happened to other people.

“Okay,” he said at last. “We’ll sit down, eat something, and wait for the light to improve before we head out.”

Jamie studied him for a moment, searching his face, then nodded. He trusted Callum. Or maybe he needed to.

They ate without appetite, the food bland and forgettable, something to occupy their hands. Outside, the wind moved along the valley in low, uneven gusts, its sound rising and falling against the stone like breath that never quite reached the room.

Jamie kept glancing at the walls, his attention snagging on the carvings that caught the firelight.

“People have stayed here before,” Callum said, following his gaze. “Old marks. Names. Dates.”

“Some of them look new,” Jamie said.

Callum didn’t answer, because there wasn’t a version of the truth that felt helpful. They packed slowly and deliberately, hands moving with care as if rushing might disturb something that was barely holding itself in place. Callum checked the map again, tracing the route with his finger until the lines blurred together.

“We’ll head out along the burn,” he said. “That’s the safest way down. It’s usually reliable.”

Jamie slung his pack over his shoulders and paused. “What if it does?”

Callum looked at him, really looked at him, at the pallor in his face and the brightness in his eyes that had nothing to do with the cold.

“We stick together,” he said. “That’s what matters.”

They stepped outside into air that bit harder than before, the cold sharp and immediate. The sky was a uniform grey, low and heavy, pressing down on the valley until the distance felt compressed and unreliable. The land felt harder to read than it should have been, distances flattening. They had been walking for the better part of fifteen minutes when Jamie stopped without warning, his sudden stillness enough to make Callum halt a step later and turn back. Jamie lifted a hand and pointed upslope, his voice low and careful when he said, “That rock.”

Callum followed the line of his finger and felt his stomach drop as the split outcrop came into view ahead of them, its jagged face unmistakable even at a distance, the recognition hitting him with a sick certainty that left no room for argument.

“That’s not possible,” he said, without much conviction, worn down by how often he’d had to repeat them since leaving the bothy.

Jamie didn’t argue. He walked ahead and rested his gloved hand against the stone, touching the strip of orange tape tied around its base and watching it flutter weakly in the breeze, as though it were trying to prove it still existed. Callum closed his eyes, long enough to steady himself.

“Okay,” he said, forcing control back into his voice. “We’re not doing this again.”

Jamie turned. “What do you mean?”

“We’re not wandering, and we’re not guessing,” Callum said. “We’re following the burn.”

Jamie glanced around the slope, scanning the land. “Where is it?”

Callum searched for the sound of moving water, something that should have been obvious in the quiet, but all he could hear was the wind slipping unevenly across the grass.

“It should be lower,” he said, without conviction.

They headed downslope, moving slowly and with care as the ground dipped and rose in awkward waves that forced them into a clumsy zigzag, and when the wind dropped without warning the silence that followed pressed hard enough against Callum’s ears that he became aware of his own breathing. Jamie’s breath sounded unnaturally loud beside him.

Then Jamie stopped short again and said, “There.”

The burn cut through the valley ahead, dark and narrow, and the sight of it sent a rush of relief through Callum.

“There we go,” he said, more to himself than to Jamie. “That’s our line.”

They followed the bank where the ground was firmer, the water moving sluggishly beside them with a dull, black surface that reflected nothing. After a while, Callum realised he had started counting steps again without meaning to, until the rhythm felt wrong.

“Jamie, do you remember that bend?” Callum asked, pointing downstream.

Jamie frowned. “Yeah.”

“We passed it already.”

Jamie looked past him, then back at the water. “No,” he said slowly. “We haven’t.”

Callum’s chest tightened as he followed the line of the burn and realised the water was flowing the wrong way.

“That’s not possible,” he said, though the phrase had lost any authority it once carried.

Jamie crouched and dipped his fingers into the water, pulling them back sharply a moment later.

“It’s warm.”

Callum stared at the surface as it rippled once, briefly.

“Back,” he said. “We’re going back to the bothy.”

Jamie flinched but nodded, and they turned uphill without another word.

The bothy wasn’t where it should have been.

They crested the rise and saw only slope and sky, and Callum stopped dead, the word “No” leaving his mouth before he’d fully understood what he was seeing.

Jamie scanned the hillside in quick, jerking movements until he spotted it farther to the right, half-hidden behind a fold of land Callum didn’t remember crossing. The building looked smaller now, lower, as if it had settled further into the hillside.

They approached in silence.

The door was closed.

Callum opened it.

Inside, the air felt thicker, heavier, as though the room resisted their presence. The stove was cold, the ash undisturbed.

“That’s not right,” Jamie said.

Callum dropped his pack, his hands shaking despite his effort to steady them.

“We’re staying,” he said.

Jamie stared at him. “What?”

“We’re staying one more night,” Callum said. “We wait for clear light, and then we leave.”

Jamie’s mouth opened and closed again. He looked suddenly very young.

“You said…”

“I know what I said,” Callum cut in, drawing a breath he didn’t feel. “I don’t like it either. But wandering around out there isn’t helping.”

Jamie sat heavily on the bench.

“This isn’t right.”

Callum didn’t deny it.

Night came early, the light bleeding out of the valley. Callum fed the stove and felt a small, shameful relief when it caught, the flame throwing long shadows across the walls that made the carvings stretch and shift. Jamie lay on his sleeping mat staring at the ceiling.

“Dad,” he said.

“Yes?”

“Did Mum ever talk about this place?”

Callum frowned. “No. Why?”

Jamie hesitated. “I thought I heard something.”

Callum’s heart lurched. “When?” he asked carefully.

“Earlier,” Jamie said. “By the burn.”

“That was the wind,” Callum said automatically, and hated himself for how fast the lie came.

Jamie didn’t argue. He turned onto his side and pulled his sleeping bag up around his shoulders.

The knocking came later, closer this time and softer. Callum sat up with his pulse hammering and whispered, “Stay still.”

The sound came again, not from the door but from somewhere behind the walls, moving along the inside of the walls.

Jamie’s breathing quickened.

“Dad,” he whispered. “It’s inside.”

Callum crossed the room and knelt beside him.

“Listen to me,” he said. “This is an old building. Sound travels. We’re tired.”

The knocking stopped, and silence settled thick and expectant around them. They lay awake for a long time. At some point Callum must have slept, because he dreamed of losing his footing and reaching for Jamie.

He woke to quiet.

The light was wrong, neither morning nor night, just a flat, colourless grey seeping through the window.

“Jamie,” he said softly.

There was no answer. He sat up. Jamie’s sleeping bag lay empty, panic hit him.

“Jamie!” he shouted, scrambling to his feet.

The door stood open.

He ran outside into a valley that lay silent and still, without wind or movement.

“Jamie!” he shouted again, and his voice fell dead in the air.

Bare footprints led away from the door, too small, stopping ten paces from the threshold.

Callum dropped to his knees, scanning the ground. There were no drag marks, no turn, no explanation. Just absence. He staggered back inside, his mind racing, grasping for order.

“Jamie,” he said again, his voice breaking.

Something moved behind him.

He turned.

Jamie stood near the far wall, half in shadow.

“There you are,” Callum breathed, relief crashing over him as he crossed the room in two strides and grabbed his son’s shoulders.

Jamie didn’t react.

“Jamie?”

His eyes lifted slowly, unfocused and distant.

“Dad,” he said. “I heard you calling.”

Callum pulled him into a hug, his hands trembling.

“Don’t do that,” he said hoarsely. “Don’t ever do that.”

Jamie stiffened in his arms.

“You shouldn’t have come.”

Callum pulled back, staring.

“What?”

Jamie blinked, as if waking.

“I said… I was cold,” he corrected too quickly. “I went outside for a second.”

Callum studied his face, but Jamie wouldn’t meet his eyes.

“Pack your things,” Callum said. “We’re leaving now.”

Jamie nodded.

As they packed, Callum noticed something carved into the wall above the hearth. A new triangle, cut cleanly into the wood, inverted.

He stared at it longer than he meant to, with the uneasy sense that he had seen it before, though he couldn’t place where.

They stepped outside together, and behind them the bothy creaked, a low, settling sound that felt uncomfortably close to satisfaction.

They left at what should have been morning, although the light never properly resolved into anything he would have called daylight, remaining pale and indistinct in a way that made time itself feel provisional. Callum packed with the same deliberate efficiency he’d relied on for most of his adult life, folding and stowing without pause, because stopping to consider what he was doing carried the risk of noticing how thin his sense of control had become. Jamie moved beside him, slower than usual, his actions careful but slightly delayed, as though each step required confirmation before it could be taken.

Callum found himself watching him more than he meant to, tracking small details with the unease that comes from sensing you’re already too late. Jamie’s hands shook when he clipped the straps of his pack, and he didn’t hum or mutter the way he normally did when concentrating. When Callum handed him his gloves, Jamie accepted them with a faint look of surprise, as though the gesture hadn’t occurred to him as something that would happen.

“Ready?” Callum asked, keeping his voice neutral.

Jamie nodded and said yes, although the word came a fraction too late.

They stepped outside together into air that was cold but utterly still, the kind of stillness that made even their breathing feel intrusive. The valley looked unchanged from the night before, pale and quiet and almost carefully arranged, with no wind moving through the grass and nothing along the ridgeline to break the flat line of sky.

Callum turned back once, unable to stop himself, and took in the bothy where it crouched against the hillside, its stone walls dull in the weak light and the door closed as though nothing inside it had ever been disturbed.

“Let’s go,” he said, more firmly than necessary.

They headed downslope along the route Callum believed would take them back toward the burn, although belief had become a fragile thing, easily bent by land that no longer behaved in ways he recognised. Jamie walked just behind him, close enough that Callum could hear his breathing, a small, steady sound that anchored him more than he liked to admit.

It took ten minutes before Callum realised something was wrong, not with the terrain this time, but with Jamie.

“Jamie,” he said, slowing. “You all right?”

Jamie stopped and said that he was, although the answer came without any of the irritation it usually carried.

Callum turned and saw how pale he looked, how his eyes failed to settle on anything for long enough to focus.

“You sure?” he asked.

Jamie hesitated, searching for the word.

“I feel tired.”

“That’s normal,” Callum said quickly, too quickly. “We didn’t sleep much.”

Jamie nodded, but the motion felt automatic, as though agreement were expected rather than believed.

They continued on, the ground sloping more steeply than Callum had anticipated, forcing him to pay close attention to his footing. He focused on the burn in his legs and the rhythm of descent, because that was easier than noticing how complete the quiet remained.

Jamie stumbled.

Callum caught him by the pack strap before he could fall, the word “Careful” coming out sharper than he intended. Jamie steadied himself and then stared down at his boots with a faint look of surprise, as if he were seeing them for the first time. He apologised, quietly.

Callum studied his face for a moment. “Do you want to stop?”

Jamie shook his head too fast. “No. I’m fine.”

The burn appeared ahead of them sooner than Callum expected, cutting a dark, narrow line through the valley, and the relief that followed loosened something in his chest he hadn’t realised he was holding. He pointed it out aloud, though Jamie didn’t respond.

Callum stopped and turned back. Jamie stood several paces behind him, staring at the water with an expression Callum didn’t recognise.

“What is it?” he asked.

Jamie frowned slightly, as though trying to retrieve a thought that had slipped out of reach.

“I don’t think we should cross it.”

Callum blinked. “Why?”

Jamie shrugged. “It feels wrong.”

Callum stepped closer, lowering his voice without quite knowing why.

“Jamie, we need to get down. That’s how this works.”

Jamie looked at him then, properly, and for a moment Callum had the unsettling impression of being examined by someone who knew him very well but no longer agreed with him.

“I don’t remember crossing it before,” Jamie said.

A chill moved through Callum that had nothing to do with temperature.

“We did yesterday.”

Jamie shook his head.

“No. I mean… I don’t remember ever crossing it.”

Callum swallowed and told himself not to overreact.

“You’re tired,” he said, offering the explanation because it was the only one he had left. “We both are.”

Jamie’s attention drifted back to the water, which moved sluggishly, its surface catching light in a way that made direction difficult to judge.

“Dad,” he said quietly. “I don’t think it wants us to leave.”

Callum tightened his grip on his poles.

“That’s enough,” he said, forcing firmness into his voice. “We’re not doing this.”

Jamie flinched, and Callum softened immediately.

“I’m sorry. But we’re going.”

He stepped into the burn, the cold water soaking into his boots at once, and turned back with his hand extended. Jamie hesitated, the silence pressing in around them until it felt close and personal.

“Jamie,” Callum said, his voice breaking despite his effort to steady it. “Please.”

After a moment that stretched too long, Jamie reached out and took his hand. His grip was colder than it should have been.

They crossed together. On the far bank Jamie pulled his hand away and stood very still, breathing hard. Callum asked if he was all right, and Jamie said yes without looking at him.

They followed the burn as it widened and quickened, the sound of water growing more familiar, more recognisable, and the land slowly began to feel less compressed, distances easing back toward something Callum could name. He allowed himself a brief hope that didn’t last long enough to settle into anything like certainty.

They rounded a bend and Jamie stopped again.

“Dad.”

Callum turned, irritation rising before he saw Jamie’s face. Jamie stood at the edge of the path, staring back the way they had come.

“I think I left something,” he said.

“What?” Callum asked, dread flaring.

Jamie shook his head. “I don’t know. It just feels like I did.”

“We’re not going back,” Callum said at once.

Jamie looked at him. “It’s important.”

Callum stepped closer and set his hands on Jamie’s shoulders.

“Listen to me. Whatever it is, it’s not more important than getting out.”

Jamie met his eyes. “What if it is?”

Callum searched his face for the boy he knew and found something quieter, older.

“We’re leaving,” he said.

Jamie’s shoulders sagged. “Okay.”

They walked on as the path narrowed and dropped, the terrain growing rougher. The strain in Callum’s legs grounded him again, and the air warmed slightly as they lost elevation.

They reached the treeline near midday.

Callum stopped.

The village lay ahead, roofs dull against the pale sky, a single thread of smoke rising. The sight struck him with physical force.

“We made it,” he said.

Jamie didn’t answer.

Callum turned. Jamie stood several paces back with his pack on the ground.

“What are you doing?” Callum asked.

Jamie looked toward the hills.

“I think I have to stay.”

Callum crossed the distance between them in two strides and grabbed his arms.

“No.”

“I don’t think it’s a choice,” Jamie said.

“You’re coming with me.”

Jamie met his eyes, recognition clear and steady for a brief, terrible moment.

“You shouldn’t have come.”

“I’m not leaving you.”

Jamie’s expression softened.

“You already did,” he said, then faltered.

“I didn’t mean…”

Callum pulled him into a fierce embrace.

“We’re going. Together.”

Jamie stiffened, then relaxed, returning the hug.

“All right.”

They walked into the village side by side. People glanced up as they passed, curiosity shifting into something else Callum didn’t care to name. He kept his hand on Jamie’s shoulder.

At the edge of the road, Jamie stopped.

“Dad.”

Callum turned. Jamie looked younger somehow.

“I don’t remember the hills,” he said. “Not really.”

“That’s okay,” Callum said. “You don’t need to.”

Jamie nodded.

“Good.”

They stepped forward.

Jamie slowed.

Callum felt it before he saw it, the sudden absence of weight at his side, the space where a shoulder should have been close enough to touch.

He turned.

“Jamie?”

The road behind them was empty.

Callum stood where he was, his hand still half-raised.

Someone would find Callum’s name written inside a bothy farther north, carved shallow and uneven into the wood near the stove.

Beneath it was a simple mark, cut once, then gone over again: a triangle, inverted.

Some places keep what they take.

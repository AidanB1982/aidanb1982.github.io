The Gåtestein Log

A recovered journal fragment, 1824.

Aidan Blackwood

The following pages were separated from the official log of the fishing vessel Gåtestein during a coastal archive review.

The handwriting has been attributed to Captain Henrik Søreide.

No official record exists of the ship’s return.

October 12

We set the nets before first light, close to the eastern wall of the fjord where the depth drops off sharp. Weather steady. No wind to speak of. The water lay flatter than I have seen it in years, though the barometer showed no cause for concern.

The stone came up in the third pull. The net snagged hard, and for a moment I thought we had caught the shelf itself. When it broke free, the weight of it dragged two men forward. It was not large, no more than what one man could lift with care, but it carried itself heavy, as if it did not wish to be moved.

At first glance it looked like bone, smoothed by long time in the water, but it was stone, dark and close-grained. One face bore markings cut by hand, a curved line worked inward upon itself. The cut was old, the edges worn, but deliberate. Anders said it was nothing worth the trouble and spat over the rail. Helvik would not touch it.

I ordered it brought aboard and lashed to the mast post until we could make shore and be rid of it.

The men grew quiet after that. Not fearful, only watchful. Work was done slower, with more looking than needful.

October 14

We have not made the distance we should have. The tide moves as expected along the rocks, but it does not take us with it. The sails hang slack though the wind comes and goes. I put it down to poor air and narrow water, but the compass has begun to wander in a way I do not like.

Sigurd stood watch last night longer than his turn and did not wake his relief. When I spoke to him, he said he was listening for something under the hull. He could not say what.

The stone remains where it was lashed. I have not ordered it moved. I find no reason for that, yet I am aware of its place without looking, as one is aware of a loose tooth.

Helvik refused to eat this morning. Said the taste of the bread had changed. I tasted none of it.

October 17

The fog came in before dawn and did not lift. It sits low and even, not drifting as it ought. Sound carries poorly within it. Orders must be repeated.

Just before first light, a vessel appeared off our port side. No oars. No wake. It lay near enough that I could see the shape of her mast and the line of her bow, but she did not answer hail. I could not see movement on her deck, though I felt watched all the same.

She did not approach, nor did she fall away. When the fog thickened again, she was no longer there.

I told the men it was a trick of weather and shadow. They did not argue, but I saw no relief in them.

The stone gave a faint tremor tonight, felt through the mast post more than heard. The men slept poorly. Anders cried out once, though he did not wake.

No Date

We remain where we were.

The water lies close and level beneath the hull. It does not answer the tide as it should. I marked the depth twice this morning and came to the same measure, though the line was let down further the second time.

The men ask how long we have been here. I told them three days. I told them four. I told them nothing when I could not be certain.

The stone is still lashed to the mast post. I know this because I have checked it. I know this because I have not checked it.

Work is done slowly. Orders must be given twice, sometimes three times, though I do not recall giving them the first.

I ordered the stone taken up and returned to the water before noon. I am certain of this.

The stone remains where it was.

No one admits to refusing the order. No one admits to hearing it. I do not recall countermanding it, but I find the thought of it being gone difficult to hold.

We have not moved.

We have not moved, as far as I can tell.

The water beneath us is patient.

I ordered the stone thrown back to the fjord at first light and watched it sink from sight. The water closed over it without sound, and I felt relief at once.

I ordered the stone thrown back to the fjord at first light. The water did not take it.

I ordered the stone thrown back to the fjord.

The waiting is the worst of it. Not fear, which comes and goes, but the sense of being held while something else takes measure. The men feel it. I feel it. Time passes, though the ship does not.

If this book is found, let it be known that we kept to our work. We did not mock what we did not understand. We listened when listening was required.

Author’s Note

This fragment forms part of a larger record connected to Lysefjord and its history. It is presented here as found, without correction or completion.

Other accounts exist, separated by time, but the water in this place has never been inclined to forget.

The events recorded here precede those documented in The Drowned Fjord.
